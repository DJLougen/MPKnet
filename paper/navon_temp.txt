ABSTRACT

Daniel J. Lougen

Master of Science in Psychology

Title: Decomposing Navon’s Forest: Dissecting the Reaction Times of
Global and Local Processing

Reaction time studies using hierarchical visual stimuli typically
demonstrate that global processing is faster than local processing
(global precedence effect), and that global interference during a local
task has a greater impact than the opposite (global interference
effect). Previous examinations of individual differences in these
reaction times have sometimes provided evidence that these effects are
modulated by various clinical conditions (e.g., autism), but these
reports are inconsistent in the literature. This may be because reaction
times are a blunt measure of processing speed, since they provide only a
sum of the durations of the many different processes that occur between
stimulus and response (including afferent transmission, decision, and
efferent transmission), and they are prone to distortion due to
speed-accuracy tradeoffs. Here, we used a compelled response task (CR)
(Stanford et al., 2010) to tease apart the duration of decision
processes from that of non-decision processes (the sum of afferent and
efferent delays). Participants viewed hierarchical C’s (large C’s made
of small c’s, both of which could be open to the right or left in a
mirror-reversed fashion) and reported their orientations at either the
global or local level (in a blocked design) with right or left button
presses. Our analysis demonstrates that decision times captured both
precedence and interference effects in visual processing. In contrast,
non-decision times were primarily influenced by the interference effect
alone. The combined measure representing the sum of decision and
non-decision components serves as a valuable comparative tool when
examined alongside standard reaction time measurements. In doing so,
demonstrating the ability of the CR to provide more precise measures of
the durations of the underlying components of a response indicates
benefits of its use over typical reaction time measures for examining
individual differences in sensorimotor processing.

Introduction

Early Hierarchical Perception

Early vision sciences were shaped by the debates between structuralists
and Gestalt psychologists. The structuralists argued that perception is
atomistic, processed from the ground up, while the Gestalt psychologists
contended that perception is contextual and holistic. Ultimately, the
Gestalt perspective prevailed, leading to an understanding of the
principles that guide how we perceptually group objects based on
similarities such as shapes and proximity (Kimchi, 1992; Wagemans et
al., 2012). This research subsequently led to the concept of perceptual
holism, where perception is more than just the individual sum of its
components. We can think of this like viewing a mosaic where each tile
contributes to the complete image.

Using this wholistic lens for perceptual analysis raised a crucial
question: “How is the information processed and in what order?” David
Navon (1977) investigated this very question and proposed that
perception processes the whole before the parts. He explained that the
perceptual system, when divided into the global (the whole) and local
(the parts) levels, processes global information before local
information. He eloquently summed this up with a metaphor: we process
the forest before the trees, where the forest represents the contextual
whole and the individual trees represent the details of the scene. One
might be inclined to assume that this is indicative of a serial process,
where global processing precedes local processing. However, Navon
emphasizes that these levels are processed in parallel within a finite
resource system, which reflect a continuous and dynamic allocation of
resources to process the hierarchy fully (Navon, 1981).

In Figure 1, the global level elements in the red boxes encapsulate
broader features, such as the forest and the dog. When viewing the local
elements in the blue boxes, we see singular elements like eyes and noses
that contribute to a face, as well as individual trees that make up the
forest. While the terms "global" and "whole" might seem synonymous at
first glance, Navon's research into perceptual processing suggests
otherwise. "Global" refers to the overarching, high-level aspects of a
stimulus, typically focusing on the larger structure or shape. In
contrast, "whole" encompasses both the global and local levels,
integrating the overall structure with the finer, detailed components.

To illustrate this further consider walking into a room where an
orchestra is playing a song. Upon initial hearing, the most salient
features that emerge are those of the orchestra as a whole. At the
global level, we effortlessly notice the harmony and overall flow of the
piece, creating a cohesive sound. However, when attempting to listen to
a single (local) instrument, the task becomes harder and requires more
focus. Notice the difficulty of each task and the resources that go into
accomplishing them: at the global level, it is effortless, while at the
local level, you find yourself straining to focus through the noise.

In the decades since Navon's original experiments, the GPE has been
robustly replicated using diverse stimuli in multiple cognitive research
domains. Where it has been used to observe how hierarchical processing
evolves in adolescence (Scherf et al., 2009) and adulthood (Scailquin,
2000; Staudinger et al., 2011a). As well as investigating individual
differences in subjects with attention hyper deficit disorder (ADHD,
Akerman et al., 2023; Yuan et al., 2024) and autism spectrum disorder
(ASD, Baisa et al., 2021; Van der Hallen et al., 2015, 2017), as well as
within the domain of religious beliefs (Colzato et al., 2010). However,
despite consistent replicability, the GPE is not always present at the
same magnitude or even at all. Naturalistic scenes are far more complex
than simple Navon letters, containing multiple levels of hierarchy,
textures, and gradients, which can confound our measurements. These
complexities add further obstacles for identification and signal
detection. Studies on non-human animals have shown that both pigeons
(Santacà, 2024; Watanabe et al., 2019) and baboons (Deruelle & Fagot,
1997; Fagot et al., 2001) demonstrate a preference for local features.
Notably, this local bias, however, is absent in other primates like
chimpanzees (Fagot et al., 2001) and macaques (Feltner & Kiorpes, 2010;
Santacà, 2024).

It is also important to consider methodological approaches, such as
visual angle, when investigating hierarchical figures. This was first
demonstrated by Kinchla and Wolfe (1979) in a target detection task
utilizing Navon letters. Participants were instructed to search for the
target letter as indicated by an audio cue and report if it was present.
They manipulated the visual angle of the global figure, with sizes
including 4.8^(°), 6.7^(°), 8.0^(°), 10.3 ^(°), or 22.1^(°). The
resulting observations suggest that the GPE is most prominent with sizes
between 6^(°) and 9^(°). But at larger visual angles, the effect
reverses creating a local precedence effect. However, Navon and Norman
(1983) argued that the effect might instead be due to differences
between foveal and peripheral processing. Their task utilized the
letters C and O to keep all information in the parafoveal field,
avoiding confounding factors such as eccentricity and globality. By
keeping eccentricity constant with identical shapes, they found that the
GPE was present in both large and small subtended angles, suggesting
that the GPE is unlikely to be due to salience at the global level.
Furthermore, stimulus composition also plays a role in perceptual bias.
For example, Martin (1979) showed that the number of elements could
modulate these biases, with a local bias evident when elements are
sparse.

Individual Differences

In contemporary research, the GPE serves as a crucial measure for
investigating how individual differences influence perception. Evidence
suggests that the GPE diminishes with age, indicating a shift in
perceptual processing priorities over the lifespan (Müller-Oehring et
al., 2007; Scailquin, 2000; Staudinger et al., 2011). Additionally, sex
differences in GPE have been shown to be influenced by hemispheric
specialization and hormonal variations, leading to distinct perceptual
patterns between males and females (Müller-Oehring et al., 2007;
Pletzer, 2014). Using Navon figures, it was found that males are faster
at identifying global figures, while females tend to use a more local
strategy associated with left hemisphere dominance (Razumnikova & Volf,
2010). More recently, Hausinger and Pletzer (2021) discovered that
levels of progesterone and testosterone influence the precedence and
interference effects. Progesterone, the primary female sex hormone, and
testosterone, the primary male sex hormone, both play significant roles
in development. Progesterone, in particular, was found to be crucial in
predicting precedence and interference effects due to hemispheric
asymmetries in males. In contrast, Voyer and MacPherson (2020) found
that males were not more efficient at holistic processing than females
regarding the precedence effect. Instead, males were better at applying
a “piecemeal strategy” using an attentional zoom to focus on local
features. Cultural backgrounds also modulate this effect; for example,
East Asians, possibly due to collectivistic societal norms, exhibit
enhanced global processing compared to their Western counterparts
(Carther-Krone & Marotta, 2022; Chua et al., 2022). Similarly, religious
affiliations, reflecting collective versus individualistic value
systems, have been associated with noticeable differences in
global-local processing, suggesting that such perceptual tendencies may
also be shaped by the underlying values attributable to religious
beliefs (Colzato et al., 2010; Hommel & Colzato, 2010). This makes it
clear that our perception is unconsciously influenced by numerous
factors of everyday life.

A significant focus has shifted towards investigating differences in
local and global processing in clinical populations, as research has
pointed toward unique perceptual accounts tied to neurodevelopmental
disorders like autism spectrum disorder (ASD) and attention hyper
deficit disorder (ADHD). ASD is a multifaceted disorder defined by
challenges in social interactions, heightened sensory sensitivity, and a
tendency towards repetitive behaviors and restricted interests (American
Psychiatric Association, 2013). ADHD is a disorder characterized by
persistent patterns of inattention, hyperactivity, and impulsivity that
interfere with functioning or development. Individuals with ADHD may
struggle with tasks requiring sustained attention, organization, and
time management, often appearing forgetful, easily distracted, or
disorganized. (American Psychiatric Association, 2013; Faraone et al.,
2021). It should be further noted that there is increasing evidence for
a notable comorbid relationship between the two. Notably, comorbidity of
the two was observed in 32.8% of children with an ASD diagnosis (Canals
et al., 2024). A meta-analysis concluded that 40.2% of ASD cases
co-presented with ADHD (Rong et al., 2021). Thus, ignoring one while
looking at the other may lead to a less thorough analysis.

Research since the 1970s has revealed a propensity for superior
visuo-spatial skills in ASD. This has been evidenced through exceptional
performance on tasks such as block design and object assembly, as
captured by the WAIS and WISC tests (Lockyer & Rutter, 1970; Tymchuk et
al., 1977). Pioneering work by Shah and Frith (1983) further illuminated
these differences, demonstrating that individuals with ASD exhibit
superior target detection in embedded figures tasks compared to
neurotypical subjects. Their subsequent research reinforced these
findings, showing enhanced spatial reasoning in unsegmented block design
tasks among ASD participants, attributed to a reduced cognitive load for
extracting local features (Shah & Frith, 1993). Studies pinpointed these
superior skills in tasks requiring detailed attention, leading to
theories such as Weak Central Coherence (WCC) and Enhanced Perceptual
Functioning (EPF), both of which suggest an atypical balance of global
and local processing. However, the theories propose that this imbalance
is instantiated in different ways – the WCC suggests a difficulty in
integrating information into a cohesive whole, a deficit in global
processing (Frith, 1989, 2003), whereas EPF posits that individuals with
ASD have heightened local processing (Mottron et al., 2006; Mottron &
Burack, 2001)

Research on the GPE reveals that individuals with ASD often experience a
significant alteration in how they process visual information, showing a
local bias or global weakness (Happé & Frith, 2006; Van der Hallen et
al., 2015). This unique perceptual orientation, which deviates from the
typical precedence given to global features, suggests a fundamental
difference in visual processing in ASD. In a meta-analysis investigating
the mixed results, Van der Hallen et al. (2015) found that, when
accounting for the level attended to, individuals with ASD show a
diminished global performance across tasks. When accounting for the
order of information presentation, this effect only appeared in
conditions where they focused on the global level and had conflicting
local information. A subsequent study by the same lab (Van der Hallen et
al., 2017) using a divided attention task found both global and local
interference effects. Further, the same data contained no indication of
significant difference between the control and ASD populations. When
investigating the same in a selective attention task they found a
typical global precedence effect, with no significant difference in
performance between subject samples. More recently, it has been
suggested that these discrepant accounts may be a result of differences
in experimental methods. When manipulating visual angle and distractor
salience, a similar typical global precedence account appeared (Baisa et
al., 2021). However, contrasting findings were seen in a figure copying
task, where longer gaze times when copying the figure suggested the
presence of a global deficit (Seernani et al., 2020). Neufeld et al.
(2020) further supports this conclusion in a study of monozygotic twins
that found a global processing deficit associated with ASD and ASD
traits. Thus, while some studies in the literature have indicated that
ASD is associated with differences in global/local processes, others
have not. In sum, the question of whether ASD is associated with
deficits in global processing, or enhancements in local processing,
remains inconclusive.

Perceptual accounts of ADHD have also been inconclusive on the question
of a relationship to global/local processing. The disorder is typically
associated with hyperactivity and difficulties in focusing on tasks and
details, leading to expectations of impaired local processing. A
substantial body of research suggests that many of these deficits
localize to the right hemisphere (Kalanthroff et al., 2013; Li et al.,
2019; Sandson et al., 2000). This finding is neurologically significant
because global information is primarily processed within the right
hemisphere, indicating hemispheric localized deficits. Key areas
implicated in this process include the right parietal lobe, right
inferior prefrontal gyrus, and right superior frontal gyrus (Brederoo et
al., 2017; A. Flevaris et al., 2010; A. V. Flevaris & Robertson, 2016;
Van Kleeck, 1989; Yovel et al., 2001). However, when assessing these
hemispheric effects behaviorally using the GPE as a measure, the results
reveal an unexpected pattern. Similar to ASD, it has been shown that
perceptual accounts of hierarchical processing in ADHD are altered in
some capacity. Some studies suggest a lack of a global bias, a reduced
global bias, or an enhanced local bias (Karalunas et al., 2014; Song &
Hakoda, 2015). An EEG study examining the GPE found significant
correlations between early neural responses associated with global
processing and deficits in both local and global processing in more
severe cases of ADHD (Yuan et al., 2024). In contrast, a previous study
using a drawing task in children found no evidence of a local bias or
differences from neurotypical controls, attributing any detected
differences to an executive functioning deficit in planning (Booth et
al., 2003). Thus, like with ASD, the literature provides hints that ADHD
is associated with differences in global/local processing, but this
assessment is far from definitive.

Limitations of Reaction Time

As mentioned earlier, cognitive sciences rely on RT as a standard
measure as it is simple and effective, a testament to the impacts made
within in the realm of cognitive neuroscience. RT has a well-established
methodological footprint offering a broad-stroke analysis that, despite
being robust, encounters limitations via the inclusion of processing
mechanisms different from the decision process. Dissecting RT reveals
three main components: afferent sensory processing, the decision, and
the efferent motor processing (Stanford et al., 2010), with afferent and
efferent processes being non-decision components of the task related to
the sensation of the stimulus and the physical button-press response,
respectively. The inclusion of non-decision processing in the RT only
serves to obscure the nuances we are trying to capture, making it clear
that more refined analyses require a more refined tool. In the past this
would have been approached using a subtractive method, which involves
the difference in RT between a simple RT task and Choice RT task
(Donders, 1969). With the difference of two being considered the
additional amount of time taken to perform the additional cognitive
operation. While the method appears intuitive at first the subtractive
method is susceptible to three drawbacks. Firstly, it is predicated on
the assumption that the cognitive resources used in both tasks are the
same. Like the difference between a task involving reacting to a green
circle appearing vs a task that asks you to press left for green and
right for red. These additional requirements add a need for
discriminatory analysis and working memory to map answers accurately to
the keys. Secondly, it also assumes that processing terminates with the
initiation of the motor action. Thirdly, it is subject to speed-accuracy
tradeoffs that come with differences in task needs (Stanford et al.,
2010). Taken together, these assumptions alone suggest that this method
is largely unsatisfactory and suffers similar blunt measure issues.

Stanford et al.'s (2010) critique of the subtraction method in RT
studies elucidates those limitations, prompting the development of the
Compelled Response (CR) paradigm as an innovative approach to better
quantify the decision process. This method addresses critical issues by
allowing for a nuanced dissection of RTs into components of decision and
non-decision time. In the CR task, the participant is required to submit
their response within a sub-1s response window, with the beginning of
the response window marked by a go cue. However, the onset of the
stimulus to which the participant must respond is not necessarily
presented immediately, but is instead presented after a pseudo-random
delay from the start of the response window. If the stimulus is
presented early in the response window, the participant will have
sufficient time to process the stimulus and initiate a fully-informed
response (with a high level of accuracy) before the response window
expires. At the other extreme, a delayed stimulus may leave insufficient
time to process the stimulus, resulting in a response that is merely a
guess, with a 50% chance of being accurate.

The tachometric curve in Figure 4 thus allows for a temporal assessment
of separate components of the participant’s sensorimotor response. In
particular, the duration at which performance is at chance (215ms,
labelled T_(Non-Decision), in the schematic of Figure 4) would reflect
the combined durations of afferent and efferent processing times, with
no impact of any decision processing. The duration of the decision
itself (labelled T_(Decision) in Figure 4) would begin at the inflection
point where accuracy first deviates away from chance performance, and
lasts until the plateau is reached (defined here by the time at which
accuracy reaches 90%, at a processing time of 300 ms). Thus, the
duration of the decision in this example is 300ms – 215ms = 85ms. The
sum of T_(Non-Decision) and T_(Decision) can be thought of as the
minimum amount of processing time that will allow for a fully informed
response to be made (which we will refer to as the time to a fully
informed response, or T_(FIR)).

The CR paradigm, therefore, represents an advancement in cognitive
neuroscience. By distinguishing between the various stages of the RT,
Stanford et al. (2010) have created a tool capable of precisely
isolating and quantifying the decision and non-decision components of
the RT within attention and decision-making studies. This approach not
only resolves some of the shortcomings of Donder’s subtraction method
but also provides a foundation for future investigations into the
temporal dimensions of cognitive processing, and a more refined
comparison of the individual differences of global/local processes and
an eventual assessment of differences in processing within clinical
populations (e.g., autistic versus neurotypical populations).

Research Questions

The primary goal of this work is to ultimately provide a measure that
isolates decision and non-decision components of the RTs in tasks
involving global and local processing. These measures offer an
opportunity to quantify decision-making tasks more cleanly without
interference from non-decision processes. Our questions are as follows:

1.  Can Stanford et al’s (2010) compelled response task provide us with
    isolated measures of decision and non-decision times in a Navon
    task?

2.  Can the isolated components allow us to better identify individual
    differences otherwise obscured by reaction time?

In doing so, we expect to demonstrate the feasibility of reducing RT to
decision and non-decision times in meaningful way. Additionally, we want
to demonstrate that these reduced measures can capture individual
differences obscured by RT. In doing so, we will demonstrate the
efficacy of the CR paradigm (Stanford et al., 2010).

Methods

Participants 

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board at the
University of Oregon. All participants provided informed written consent
before taking part in the study. Our cohort consisted of 23 individuals,
of which 13 were female, with an average age of 22.48 years (ranging
from 18 to 35 years). Criteria for inclusion required participants to
have normal or corrected-to-normal vision, and all were recruited from
the University of Oregon community. Participants ran seven 1-hour
sessions, with compensation structured to increase with each hour spent
on the study, starting at $10.00 per hour for the initial session. Each
subsequent hour of participation saw an increase of $1.00 per hour,
capping at $16.00 per hour for the final session. In instances where
technical difficulties, such as computer crashes, necessitated
additional time, participants were compensated above the $16.00 per hour
rate to account for the extra time spent. Technical issues with the
PsychoPy program resulted in data loss for 2 participants, requiring the
re-collection of data for the affected sessions. Attrition among the
participants resulted in 7 participants not completing the seven
sessions – their data were excluded from the final analyses. Another 2
participants failed to reach a threshold performance level of 90%
correct across all RT sessions, and their data were also excluded from
the final analyses.

Apparatus 

The experiment was completed in an isolated room on a Mac mini 2020 (M1
processor, 8GB memory, Ventura 13.4 OS). Stimuli were displayed on a
24-inch Zowie XL LCD monitor (1024 X 576 resolution, 120hz refresh
rate). Participants were seated with their heads placed on a chin and
forehead rest positioned 22.5-inches from center of the screen. Lastly,
the task was coded and executed using PsychoPy v2022.2.1.

Stimuli

Hierarchically structured Navon letters were created to place both
global and local elements in the perifoveal field, ensuring that
information at both the local and global levels were equidistant from
the fovea. Each stimulus consisted of a large C formed by smaller Cs
(see Figure 5), which varied the opening in vertical or horizontal
orientation according to the experimental condition. Participants were
instructed to attend to either the large C (global level) or the smaller
Cs (local level) and respond based on the left/right orientation of the
C's opening (see Procedures below). Conditions included congruent
(matching orientations) and incongruent (opposing orientations) stimuli.
Control stimuli were also presented: during global-level control trials,
the small Cs were oriented vertically, and in local-level control
trials, the large C was oriented vertically, thus were neither congruent
nor incongruent within the participant’s task of reporting the
left/right orientation of the attended level.

General Procedure

Participants were asked to complete seven sessions in total: one RT
session and six CR sessions. Each session contained eight experiment
blocks, with each block comprised of at least 150 trials (see below).
Each block of trials required the participant to attend either the
global or local level of the Navon figure, with the first block always
requiring attention to the global level and then alternating between the
local and global levels after that. Prior to starting the first session
(an RT session) and the second session (the initial CR session),
participants were provided a practice block of trials to learn task
requirements, with enough trials (ranging from 20 to 50) for the
participant to become comfortable with the task. Within each session,
short breaks were provided between blocks, and a longer break was
provided after the 4^(th) block. In addition, on any given trial,
participants could choose not to respond at all if they felt themselves
distracted from the task – after a delay of two seconds with no
response, the program went into a break routine that allowed the
participant to refocus their attention on the task before beginning
again by pressing any key. The trials that received no response in this
way were replayed later in the experiment.

Reaction Time Task

The first session was an RT decision task, in which the participant was
asked to respond to the orientation of the Navon figure at the attended
level using the ‘f’ or ‘j’ key to indicate the orientation (left or
right, respectively) of the opening in the large C (global level) or
small C’s (local level). Each trial began with a fixation point,
followed by a click and the appearance of the stimulus (centered around
and replacing the fixation point), with the orientation of the C (or
C’s) at the attended level chosen randomly and the orientation of the C
(or C’s) at the unattended level pseudo-randomly oriented in a
congruent, incongruent or control direction (with equal numbers of each
in each block). The participants were instructed to answer, “as quickly
and as accurately as possible”.

Before analyzing the RT data, reaction times were filtered for outliers
that were ±3 SD from the median. Mean RTs were then compared across
conditions, to quantify any precedence and interference effects.

Compelled Response Task

After completion of the RT session, subsequent sessions tested the
participant’s performance in a CR task, which leveraged the same basic
set up as the RT task. CR trials (Figure 6) differed from the RT trials
in that the participant was required to respond within a 700ms temporal
response window (the CR window, which began with the offset of the
fixation point and whose duration was defined by a series of fours
clicks presented 0ms, 233.3ms, 466.6ms, and 700ms from fixation offset).
In addition, the Navon stimulus was presented with a variable delay (0m,
60ms, 133ms, 200ms, 267ms, 333ms, 400ms, and 467ms) after the onset of
the CR window, which allowed the participant a variable amount of time
to process the stimulus before needing to respond by the end of the CR
window. We incentivized our participants to answer during the response
window via a points system: 2 points for correct responses that occurred
during the CR window, 0 points for incorrect responses that occurred
during the CR window, and –1 points, paired with a an audible warning
(siren sound), for responding after the CR window ended.

To characterize the relationship between PT and accuracy, we fit a
tachometric curve (Stanford et al., 2010) to the data for each
participant, separately for each task and congruency condition. This
curve provides a method of quantifying the time course of the decision
process, specifically allowing a calculation of the durations of the
decision and nondecision components of the task. The curve itself is
calculated using a modified exponential function that assumes that
performance will be at chance with very short PTs, followed by an
increase in accuracy that exponentially approaches an asymptote level of
good performance with longer PTs:

For PT ≤ T_(Non − Decision), f(Acc) = 0.5;

$$For\ PT > T_{Non - Decision},\ f(Acc) = 0.5 + \left( {Acc}_{meanRT} - 0.5 \right)*(1 - \exp\left( \frac{- \left( PT - T_{Non - Decision} \right)}{Tau} \right)\ $$

Where f(Acc) is the tachometric curve’s model time course of accuracy,
PT is the processing time for individual trials, T_(Non-Decision) is the
non-decision time (sum of the afferent and efferent delays, depicted
here as the processing time at which accuracy begins to deviate from
chance), and Tau is the time constant of the exponential curve. Since
the proportion of correct trials in the RT data was rarely 1.0, it could
not be assumed that the plateau accuracy in the CR task would be 1.0;
instead, we assumed that the plateau accuracy in the different
combinations of Task (global, local) and Congruency (congruent, control,
incongruent) of CR tasks would equal the average accuracy of that same
trial type in the RT task (Acc_(meanRT)). To determine the values of
T_(Non-Decision) and Tau, we used the ‘optim’ function from the ‘Stats’
package in R (R Core Team, 2013) to iteratively fit the tachometric
function to the data. To avoid local minima, this fit procedure was
completed using random starting values 1000 times for each trial
condition, keeping the resulting set of parameters that led to the
lowest sum of squared errors. Although T_(Non-Decision) provides a
direct measure of the combined afferent and efferent delays, Tau (which
represents the time required for the exponential rise in accuracy to
move from chance to 75%) must be multiplied by a factor of 1.61 to
determine the threshold at which accuracy surpasses 90%; therefore,
T_(Decision) = Tau * 1.61. T_(FIR) (the time to a fully informed
response, or the minimum processing time required to make a response
with an accuracy greater than 90%) is therefore the sum of the
T_(Non-Decision and) T_(Decision).

Results

Reaction Time Task

The RT task was designed to capture mean RT for each trial type within
participants (Table 1) and to quantify the GPE and GIE, allowing for
comparisons with traditional global-local processing effects found in
the literature. Broadly, the data aligns with Navon’s (1977) findings,
demonstrating faster reaction times (RTs) for global tasks compared to
local tasks, and a larger interference from extraneous global
information in a local task than vice-versa (Figure 8A).

To further investigate the effects of task type and congruency on mean
RT, a two-way ANOVA (Task × Congruency) was performed. Results showed a
significant main effect of Task, F(1,22)=123.18, p<0.001, ηp²=0.85, 95%
CI [0.71, 0.91], with faster RT in the globally-directed task compared
to the locally-directed task. This main effect of Task explained a
substantial portion of the variance in mean RT. A significant main
effect of Congruency was also observed, F(2,44)=28.99, p<0.001,
ηp²=0.57, 95% CI [0.36, 0.70]. RT differed across congruency conditions,
with congruent trials producing faster RTs compared to both control and
incongruent trials, indicating that congruency influences RT
independently of task type. Moreover, a significant Task × Congruency
interaction was found, F(2,44)=8.36, p=0.0008, ηp²=0.28, 95% CI [0.06,
0.46]. This interaction, in tandem with main effects, suggests that Task
type is the primary driver of differences in RT, with Congruency effects
modulated by whether participants are attending to global or local
information. Specifically, the impact of Congruency is more pronounced
within local tasks than global tasks, indicating that task demands play
a critical role in how congruency influences response times.

For a more direct comparison to the experimental data, a two-way ANOVA (Task × Congruency) was performed with participants who completed the compelled response task. Results showed a significant main effect of Task, F(1, 13) = 105.74, p < .0001, ηp² = 0.89, 95% CI [0.73, 0.94], with reaction times differing significantly between task conditions. A significant main effect of Congruency was also observed, F(2, 26) = 19.99, p < .0001, ηp² = 0.61, 95% CI [0.33, 0.75], indicating that response times varied across congruency conditions. The Task × Congruency interaction approached significance, F(2, 26) = 2.88, p = .074, ηp² = 0.18, 95% CI [0.00, 0.41]. 

[]

  Traditionally these RT differences are more specifically quantified
  through the measures of GPE and GIE. The magnitude of the GPE is
  typically assessed through a direct comparison of the RTs in the
  control conditions, to avoid the possible confounds of congruent and
  incongruent information. It is calculated as follows:

  GPE = Local control RT − Global control RT

  In the present study, responses guided by local information were, on
  average, 45 ms slower than those guided by global information, and
  this difference was statistically significant, as assessed with a
  Welch’s t-test (t(43.88) = -2.58, p = 0.0135). The effect size for
  this difference was Cohen’s d = -0.78 (95% CI: [-1.39, -0.16],
  suggesting that the observed difference in reaction times confirms the
  strong presence of a GPE in our RT task.

  The GIE is typically quantified in the literature as a difference of
  differences, defined as:

  GIE= (Local incongruent RT − Local congruent RT) − (Global incongruent RT − Global congruent RT)

  In the current study, the first part of the equation represents the
  impact of extraneous global information on RT when the task requires
  attending to local information, with the global information speeding
  RTs by 10 ms (STATS – is the benefit alone significant?) when it is
  congruent with the local information, or slowing RTs by 12 ms (STATS –
  is the benefit alone significant?) when it is incongruent, resulting
  in a difference of 23 ms in our data. The second part of the equation
  represents the impact of extraneous local information on RT when the
  task requires attending to global information, with the local
  information speeding RTs by 7 ms (STATS – is the benefit alone
  significant?) when it is congruent with the local information, or
  slowing RTs by 2 ms (STATS – is the cost alone significant?) when it
  is incongruent, resulting in a difference of 9 ms. The 14 ms
  difference between these values, the GIE, is statistically
  significant, as assessed with a Welch’s t-test t(112.61) = -5.45, p <
  0.000001, with an effect size of Cohen’s d = -1.03 (95% CI: [-1.42,
  -0.63]).

  The GPE and GIE measured in the present study are smaller than those
  reported by Navon (1977), who found a GPE of 110 ms and GIE of 76 ms,
  they were nonetheless significant. This discrepancy may be
  attributable to methodological differences, such as the letters used,
  stimulus size or retinal eccentricity.

Analysis of Parameters

A repeated-measures ANOVA was conducted to examine the main effects of
Task and Congruency, as well as their interaction, on each parameter.

Non-Decision Time (T_(Non-Decision)) Analysis

The analysis of T_(Non-Decision) (Figure 8B) revealed a statistically
significant main effect of Task, F(1,13)=10.54 , p=.006, partial
η²=0.45, 95% CI [0.06,0.70], indicating that non-decision times differed
significantly between global and local tasks. There was also a
significant main effect of Congruency, F(2,26)=7.44, p=.003, partial
η²=0.36, 95% CI [0.07,0.58], suggesting that non-decision times varied
across the congruent, control, and incongruent conditions. Additionally,
a significant Task × Congruency interaction was found, F(2,26)=10.18,
p<.001, partial η²=0.44, 95% CI [0.13,0.63], indicating that the effect
of Congruency on non-decision time was moderated by the task type.

[]

To test for the presence of a GPE within the non-decision time,
T_(Non-Decision) in the global control task was subtracted from that of
the local control task for each participant. The mean GPE across
participants was found to be 11 ms, but a Welch’s t test found the
effect to be non-significant, t(20.39)=−1.63, p=.1195, d=−0.72, 95% CI
[−1.61,0.18]. In contrast, the effect of congruency on T_(Non-Decision)
in the local task was substantially greater than in the global task,
yielding a significant GIE of 48 ms, t(57.06) = −2.98, p=.0043, d=−0.79,
95% CI [−1.32,−0.25]. These results indicate that although the efferent
and afferent delays of sensorimotor processing in the CR task do not
differ between the local and global tasks, the impact of irrelevant
global information in a local task is greater than that of irrelevant
local information in a global task. This effect is particularly evident
in the marked increase in non-decision time in the local incongruent
condition, as illustrated in Figure 8B.

Decision Time (T_(Decision)) Analysis

An analysis of T_(Decision) (Figure 8C) revealed a statistically
significant main effect of Task, F(1,13)=20.98, p=.0005, partial
η²=0.62, 95% CI [0.22,0.80], indicating that decision times differed
significantly between the global and local task conditions. There was
also a significant main effect of Congruency, F(2,26)=6.86 , p=.004,
partial η²=0.35, 95% CI [0.05,0.56], suggesting that decision times
varied across the congruent, control, and incongruent conditions. The
Task × Congruency interaction, however, was not statistically
significant, F(2,26)=2.56, p=.097, partial η²=0.16, 95% CI [0.00,0.40],
indicating that while there was some interference caused by the
unattended elements in both tasks, the magnitude of the interference was
independent of the attended level.

A more direct quantification of the GPE on decision time was made by
directly comparing T_(Decision) in the local control and global control
tasks. Across participants, decision times were found to be 32 ms longer
in the local control task than in the global control, t(18.61) = -3.10,
p = .00599, d = -1.44, 95% CI [-2.44, -0.40], indicating a robust GPE.
Similarly, a pronounced GIE effect of 22 ms was observed (t(54.89) =
-4.62, p = .00002, d = -1.25, 95% CI [-1.82, -0.66]), with a greater
impact of the irrelevant global information on a local task than
vice-versa.

Time to a Fully Informed Response (T_(FIR)) Analysis

An analysis of T_(FIR) (Figure 8D) yielded a statistically significant
main effect of Task, F(1,13)=69.09, p<.001, partial η²=0.84, 95% CI
[0.61,0.92], indicating that the time to form a fully informed response
(the sum of the non-decision and decision times) differed significantly
between the global and local levels. There was also a significant main
effect of Congruency, F(2,26)=40.21, p<.001, partial η²=0.76, 95%
[0.56,0.85], suggesting that T_(FIR) varied across congruent, control,
and incongruent conditions. Additionally, a significant Task ×
Congruency interaction was found, F(2,26)=31.37, p<.001, partial
η²=0.71, 95% CI [0.48,0.82], indicating that the effect of Congruency on
T_(FIR) was moderated by the task type.

The analysis of T_(FIR) revealed significant global precedence and
interference effects. The robust GPE was evidenced by the finding that
the local control trials took 43 ms longer to reach an accuracy of 90%
compared to the global control trials, t(19.18) = -3.83, p = .00112, d =
-1.44, 95% CI [-2.44, -0.40]. Similarly, a substantial GIE was observed,
with the impact of irrelevant global information on a local task 70 ms
than the reverse, t(50.94) = -6.32, p < .00000007, d = -1.77, 95% CI
[-2.41, -1.12]. These findings demonstrate that the processing time
required to allow for a fully informed response is influenced by both
precedence and interference effects.

If RT were used alone, both precedence and interference effects would be
observed, but their contributions would be limited to confirming their
presence. By analyzing the parameters and curves, however, we can derive
more detailed insights beyond the mere identification of these effects.
This approach enables us to attribute differences in RT more precisely
by disentangling them into decision and non-decision components, thereby
providing a deeper and more nuanced understanding of the global
precedence hypothesis.

In the case of each subject, we see that references to the mean RT do
yield useful information. But do not deliver on the quantity or quality
of data that one captures with CR derived parameters. Within each
participant’s RT we detected precedence effects well and interference to
a lesser degree. When using the parameters we see that the precedence
and interference effects are detected and show varying degrees of those
effects unique to that subject. Demonstrating that individual
differences are capable of obscuring present effects in mean RT. In the
case of ASD, we can better approach prior inconclusive findings with a
new light. Where, for example, we might find non-decision times reflect
a deficit and the presence of a local bias in decision time obscures it.
Understanding the structural components of our metrics is imperative if
we want to examine the complexity of intra-individual differences.

General Conclusions

Since its inception in experimental psychology, RT has served as a gold
standard measure across various paradigms, showcasing versatility and
simplicity that have allowed it to remain relevant in modern science.
However, like any measure, RT has its limitations, particularly its
tendency to obscure observed relationships by conflating decision and
non-decision processes. Utilizing the CR paradigm designed by Stanford
et al. (2010) to investigate the global precedence effect, we
demonstrate that RT can be decomposed into its constituent components
using variable gaps, enabling us to identify the distinct time courses
of non-decision and decision processes. Through the use of the
psychometric function, we quantified individual variability in decision
parameters across task conditions (global and local) and congruency
conditions (congruent, control, incongruent). Similar to RT, we detected
both precedence and interference effects consistent with Navon’s work,
but with greater variability in the measures of non-decision and
decision time.

Overall, the parameters demonstrate a superior ability to mean RT in
their effectiveness at capturing precedence and interference effects.
Non-decision times, for example, emphasize differences that stem from
conflicting information rather than the attended level, offering deeper
insights into the underlying cognitive mechanisms. However, it does not
capture the extent of the precedence effect in the same way decision
time does. Where decision time captures both a global precedence and
interference effects. In contrasting the different parameters, we are
able to see that the global precedence effect is a processing related
component. Whereas the global interference effect manifests in when we
begin and how it takes for us to process the stimulus.

This level of precision is particularly critical when examining
individual differences, such as in individuals with ASD, where the
subtlety of these parameters can determine whether key effects are
detected or overlooked. Each parameter, when examined comparatively,
shows unique interference and precedence patterns that allow for
localizing effects to specific processing components. However, direct RT
comparisons are most informative when parameters are combined. The
cumulative metric, T_(FIR), stands out as better candidate for a
comparative metric due to its ability to minimize noise and capture both
effects in a more general comparable way to mean RTs.

Limitations 

While working to decompose RT, we encountered several challenges in
conducting the experiment. First, the study was hindered by design flaws
related to the scope and demands of data collection. As previously
mentioned, a dataset was considered complete and analyzable only after
participants completed several 1-hour sessions. The high number of
trials per set further complicated retention, as a single block could
last approximately 15 minutes, requiring participants to maintain
focused attention throughout. These cognitive and time demands were less
than ideal for participants, leading to the loss of 3–4 additional
participants during data collection. Consequently, the lower sample size
limited our ability to make more confident claims about the relationship
between RT and CR measures.

We also found that the psychometric function struggled to optimally
capture curve fits when performance dipped below 50% accuracy. This
resulted in tachometric curves that began after observable data shifts
and produced tau values that reached maximum accuracy within 10 ms. This
discrepancy highlights the limitations of the method in accurately
modeling data at low accuracy levels.

Future Experiments

One future avenue of exploration is modifying the task with the
inclusion of a touch screen to replace simple binary key presses. The
experiment begins with participants moving their finger, on the screen,
upward to a go target. The experiment is the same, except the go signal
is initiated by the participant with an upward trace to a go target.
Upon touching the target, the stimulus will appear variably, and they
will have to trace their finger as a response to orientation at the
attended level. This experiment allows us to quantify the processing of
the stimulus in such a way that individual trials can indicate when
processing begins. One major benefit of this design is the ability to
scale down the number of trials needed for a participant. Doing so
allows us to decrease the time and mental demands of the task as the
current form is often reported to be tiring after each session. One
other complication this resolve is the allocation of funding allowing
for more data to be collected using less trials and funding. This design
also benefits from real time tracking of the decision process via the
tracing of the response giving both position and velocity metrics that
can additionally evaluate the process.

The drift diffusion model (DDM), introduced by Ratcliff (1978), provides
a detailed theoretical framework for understanding decision-making as an
evidence accumulation process. This model incorporates several critical
parameters that elucidate the mechanisms underlying decisions.

Firstly, bias reflects a predisposition towards one choice over another.
In the context of hierarchical processing, such as the global precedence
effect, bias represents a tendency to prioritize global over local
information. For instance, in global tasks, the bias parameter is
expected to be closer to the correct response, whereas in local tasks,
it may be more distant, reflecting a reduced predisposition towards
global information.

Secondly, non-decision time accounts for the initial period prior to the
commencement of evidence accumulation. This parameter is particularly
relevant for distinguishing preparatory processes from the
decision-making process itself.

Thirdly, the drift rate serves as a key parameter that indicates the
speed at which evidence is accumulated toward making a decision. A
higher drift rate denotes faster accumulation of evidence, leading to
more rapid decision-making. This parameter effectively captures the
efficiency and speed with which information is processed and integrated
during the decision-making process.

Finally, the threshold parameter determines the amount of evidence
required to reach a decision. In tasks involving global precedence, a
bias towards global features might result in a lower threshold for
decisions about global elements, indicating that less evidence is needed
to commit to such responses. Conversely, tasks emphasizing local
features may require higher thresholds, reflecting greater caution or
deliberation in decision-making.

The DDM offers significant advantages by dissecting the components of
decision-making, thereby providing insights into the temporal and
accuracy-based aspects of decision processes. Similar to the tachometric
curve, this model isolates distinct stages of decision-making,
mitigating the limitations of traditional reaction time measures that
often obscure nuanced cognitive processes. By elucidating how evidence
is accumulated and identifying the parameters that modulate this
process, the DDM serves as a comprehensive tool for investigating
cognitive mechanisms. This is particularly pertinent in tasks requiring
differentiation between global and local processing and offers potential
for assessing individual differences, particularly in clinical
populations where subtle variations in cognitive processing are critical
to understanding behavioral outcomes.
